# Beat Cricinfo on trust, not on volume

Cricinfo's most useful lessons for SCRBRD lie below the match page, and most of them show what to do better rather than what to copy. On retention, Cricinfo keeps readers through a **fixture rhythm**: a preview, a written live report at the breaks, the result and a weekly podcast, all carried by alerts that reviewers call reliable. It loses readers when it pushes matches they never followed, or when an update adds ads. On data, Cricinfo's live record is an **editorial transcription** that sits downstream of the official scorers. In South Africa the boards' official ball-by-ball comes from Opta, Cricket South Africa's data partner. Cricinfo publishes no correction policy and shows no "amended" mark. Even its own data records line and length for **under half its balls**, and the weights behind its derived metrics are unpublished, which draws criticism. When a school appoints the scorer, SCRBRD's event log can be the official record, and it can show its source, coverage and corrections openly, which none of the major sites found does. On children, Cricinfo covers youth cricket only from U19 internationals upward, and treats those teenagers like professionals. Every grassroots platform found shows names by default, and privacy is something a family has to switch on. SCRBRD's rule of no child's name or photo on a public page is stricter than all of them, and it matches CSA's own treatment of minors' data as special personal information. On money, SuperSport Schools gives school video away free and a mostly-child audience rules out behavioural ads, so SCRBRD's revenue has to come from schools or a union or federation contract. SCRBRD's larger exposure is not a missing feature. It lies in what other parties already publish about the same pupils, and in third-party vendors, which is where cricket's data breaches have actually happened.

**About the evidence.** This machine's network policy blocked direct fetches of almost every site involved, including cricinfo.com and espncricinfo.com, the app stores, PlayHQ's and Play-Cricket's help centres, CricClubs and Wikipedia. **Most sources in this report were therefore read as search-engine result summaries and snippets, not as full pages.** Treat figures as indicative, and recheck any one that a decision will rest on once those hosts are allowed. Where sources conflict, or a claim comes from a weak source, the report says so. One context fact matters for every citation: on 23 June 2026 JioStar renamed ESPNcricinfo back to **Cricinfo**, and old links redirect ([exchange4media](https://www.exchange4media.com/industry-briefing-news/espncricinfo-to-be-rebranded-as-cricinfo-155697.html); [JioStar](https://cdn.jiostar.com/jiostar/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/ESPNcricinfo-is-back-to-being-Cricinfo.pdf)). This report builds on the two audits of 23 October 2026 (`audit/ESPNCRICINFO_FULL_UX_AUDIT_2026-10-23.md` and `audit/ESPNCRICINFO_LESSONS_AUDIT_2026-10-23.md`) and does not repeat what they cover.

## Cricinfo keeps readers with a fixture rhythm, and loses them with noise

The audits covered per-match alert toggles and favourites. The 2025–26 evidence adds a sharper point: users complain less about how fine-grained the alerts are than about **alerts they never chose**. One reviewer "temporarily deleted ESPNcricinfo … as it is sending notifications for games I didn't request," and a 2026 comparison says promotional pushes cannot be muted without also losing score alerts ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/417408017/cricinfo-live-cricket-scores/reviews); [Unstar](https://unstar.app/blog/cricbuzz-espncricinfo-hotstar-fancode-crex-cricket-apps-ranked-2026)). The one alert quality reviewers praise is that live-match notifications are "very reliable" ([AppFollow](https://appfollow.io/ios/preloaded/417408017?country=us)). Together these mean that, for SCRBRD's notifications design, a child's own match, the team sheet, logistics and school news should be **separate channels, each switchable on its own**. Nothing should ever be pushed for a fixture the user did not follow. A late notice should also be marked late, not sent as if it were live. One correction to the audits belongs here too. The Lessons audit says users who personalise "visit more than three times as often." A targeted search found **no source for that figure**, so it should be marked unverified before a design doc quotes it.

Cricinfo's habit loop follows the fixture calendar, not a daily feed. For the 2025–26 Ashes each Test had a `/live-match-blog`, with bylined "Live Report" segments filed through the day: a session story, not ball-by-ball ([1st Test live blog](https://www.espncricinfo.com/series/the-ashes-2025-26-1455609/australia-vs-england-1st-test-1455611/live-match-blog-2); [5th Test live report](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/australia-vs-england-5th-test---live-report---spin-sidelined-again-at-scg-1517975)). The **Switch Hit** podcast adds a weekly rhythm ([Apple Podcasts](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/id304534298)). No official Cricinfo newsletter or WhatsApp channel turned up, though rivals such as The Cricketer run WhatsApp channels ([The Cricketer](https://thecricketer.com/Topics/news/how_to_join_the_cricketer's_brilliant_new_whatsapp_channel.html)). The live report's shape suits the parent who arrives at tea. SCRBRD's version is a short "story so far" at drinks, the innings break and the close, built from the event log or written by the team manager, with no child named on public pages. SCRBRD's loop is the **fixture week**: team sheet, reminder, live, result. Each step gives a reason to come back without any need for ads or a large volume of content.

Cricinfo's derived metrics also bring readers back, mainly as **stories**. At the T20 World Cup 2026 semi-final a news outlet ran a Forecaster swing as a headline, "India's win probability rose from 77.72% to 89.88% after Jasprit Bumrah's 18th over" ([Inshorts](https://inshorts.com/en/amp_news/india-s-win-probability-rose-from-77-72--to-89-88--after-jasprit-bumrah-s-18th-over-vs-england-1772765644322)). Cricinfo itself publishes contrarian pieces such as "Why Pat Cummins, and not Shubman Gill, is the Smart Stats player of the match" ([Cricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/print/story/1233574)). The Full audit (§10.5) already rules out an uncalibrated win percentage for U13 cricket. The pull lies in the moment, though, not the model. "The over that turned it" or "the partnership that won it", drawn from SCRBRD's event log, gives the same reason to share without making a prediction about children.

Cricinfo has also **dropped open participation**, and that is instructive in its own right. Super Selector, Open Mic live chat, reader Q&As and in-app commentary feedback are all historical. An editor wrote that one of the biggest problems with live online discussion is "the sheer volume of feedback" ([Wikipedia: Super Selector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN_Super_Selector); [afaqs: Open Mic](https://www.afaqs.com/companies/espncricinfo-launches-an-innovative-live-chat-show-open-mic); [Cricinfo, From the Editor](https://www.espncricinfo.com/page2/content/story/614802.html)). If a media company with paid staff found moderation too costly, open comments on matches involving minors would carry that cost plus a safeguarding risk. The participation idea worth taking is **AskCricinfo** (2021), a natural-language question box over Statsguru ("most sixes in powerplay in IPL 2021") ([Adgully](https://www.adgully.com/espncricinfo-launches-game-changing-new-ai-powered-query-service-108582.html?amp=1)). Scoped by role and policy to a school's own figures, it engages people without any need for moderation. Its current status at Cricinfo is unknown.

Finally, trust drains through updates and slowness. A May 2026 review says the ads "became awful after the last update," and others say they moved to Cricbuzz ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/417408017/cricinfo-live-cricket-scores/reviews)). Cricinfo's March 2026 release notes headline "faster live scores & commentary with reduced lag during high-traffic moments," which suggests lag at peak load was a known problem ([APKMirror](https://www.apkmirror.com/apk/jiostar-india-private-limited/espncricinfo-live-cricket-scores-news-videos/)). Cricbuzz, India's most-visited sports site at about **192M visits in October 2025** by Semrush's estimate, is consistently judged the faster app ([Semrush](https://cw.semrush.com/website/cricbuzz.com/overview); [Medium comparison](https://medium.com/new-media-dod/espn-cricinfo-vs-cricbuzz-b4086a9efd64)). In this category, readers choose the app they think is faster, not the one with more depth. SCRBRD's peak load is a tight final over with every parent refreshing at once, so that is the moment to load-test. A "no new interruptions" rule for each release protects the parent's expectation that the score page will look the same on Saturday.

## Cricinfo transcribes the match; a SCRBRD scorer can keep the official book

Cricinfo says almost nothing in public about who enters its ball-by-ball, with what tool, how quickly, or how it is reconciled with the official scorers. No primary source turned up. The only descriptions are a forum claim, which is hearsay, that commentary is typed "in front of a TV in their office in Bangalore" ([CricketWeb forum](https://www.cricketweb.net/forum/threads/what-is-the-criteria-for-being-a-commentator-on-cricinfos-match-commentary.78024/)), and a spam-grade page claiming "150 accredited scorers", which should not be repeated. The industry's official pipeline sits elsewhere. **Stats Perform (Opta) is the official data partner of the ICC, the ECB, Cricket Australia, New Zealand Cricket and Cricket South Africa**, and collects ball-by-ball on over five million deliveries a year ([Intergame](https://www.intergameonline.com/sports-betting/news/optas-hold-on-cricket-data-extended)). In England, CricViz analysts collect in the ground at more than 500 matches a summer ([CricViz](https://cricviz.com/data-collection/)). Cricinfo's live record is therefore a **media product**, and the authority sits with the match's scorers and the board's data partner.

SCRBRD's position is the reverse, and stronger. When the school appoints the scorer, SCRBRD's event log **is** the scorebook. The provenance model should say so plainly: each match marked `official`, `unofficial` or `imported`, with the appointed scorer's role on the record. The industry also separates the person who records the fact (the scorer or analyst) from the person who writes the story (the commentator). SCRBRD's volunteer does both on one phone, so the data has to keep the split instead. Event fields hold the fact and free-text notes hold the story, so a change to a note never changes the score, and a change to the score always goes through an amendment. Because Opta holds CSA's data, school cricket sits outside the federation's official feed. The gap is SCRBRD's to fill, and any future link to CSA pathway data would run through that partner.

On corrections, **no published Cricinfo correction policy, scorecard changelog or "amended" marker was found**. ESPN has a help page titled "Scoring & Stat Corrections," but its content could not be retrieved ([ESPN Support](https://support.espn.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000099732-Scoring-Stat-Corrections)). Cricinfo's best-documented "corrections" happen at the level of the archive, and they show that cricket's records carry a third layer of truth that SCRBRD's design should model. Cricinfo credits W. G. Grace with **124 first-class centuries**, following CricketArchive, where Wisden long said 126 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_first-class_cricket_centuries_by_W._G._Grace)). When Wisden aligned with the Association of Cricket Statisticians and Historians (ACS) in 2022, Grace's totals fell from **54,896 runs and 2,876 wickets to 54,211 and 2,809**, because ten of his matches were judged not first-class. No ball had been re-scored ([Wisden](https://www.wisden.com/wisden-cricketers-almanack/wisden-aligns-with-acs-great-accord-almanack)). The ICC re-rules status too: in 2021 it gave women's cricket first-class status retrospectively ([Wikipedia: First-class cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-class_cricket)).

That gives three layers. The **events** are what happened. The **official score** is what the scorers signed. The **classification** is what the match counts as, and under whose ruling. SCRBRD's amendments and the GA-I36 "corrections everywhere" design already handle the first two. The third argues for keeping each fixture's competition, class (competitive, friendly, inter-house) and eligibility for records as **versioned data, separate from the events**, with who ruled and when. Re-ruling a fixture then changes records without touching the event log, and a records page can say "as classified by <authority> on <date>". When an imported paper scorebook and a live log disagree, the Wisden–ACS pattern says to keep both and state which one is authoritative, rather than silently overwrite one with the other.

Targets and results need the same treatment. Under the DLS conventions a rain-affected result "must be noted and shown after the result," as in "won by 11 runs (DLS Method)." The DLS report must be kept even if the match ends as no result ([Cricket NSW DLS handout](https://nswcusa.cricketnsw.com.au/app/uploads/2020/05/DLS-v4.0-2020-Instructional-Handout_202204.pdf); [Cricket Ireland DLS protocol](https://cricketireland.ie/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/DLS3_protocol_September_2018.pdf)). A Bangladesh T20I shows a target changing twice in one chase, from none to 170 to 171 ([Wisden](https://wisden.com/cricket-news/t20i-delayed-in-chaotic-circumstances-after-bangladesh-begin-chase-without-dls-target)). Within SCRBRD-114's single playing-conditions model, a target or result should be a **versioned event** (provisional, revised, confirmed), not a field that gets overwritten. The trust signals Cricinfo leaves undocumented cost SCRBRD very little, because they come straight from its event log. "Scored live by school scorer · last ball 14:32", "Imported from scorebook photo · checked by team manager", "Result confirmed by teacher in charge" and "Corrected 16:05: wrong bowler" would each be a short line on the scorecard. Each names a **role**, never a pupil scorer, in keeping with `docs/policy/PUBLIC_DATA.md`.

## Half the rich fields are empty, even at Cricinfo

Cricinfo's archive is the closest precedent for SCRBRD's scorebook importer. Both turn paper into structured scorecards. In the 1990s volunteers keyed old scorecards from print sources such as Wisden, and Statsguru grew out of one volunteer's work on that database ([Wikipedia: Cricinfo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricinfo); [Wikipedia: Travis Basevi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travis_Basevi)). Every Test scorecard was online by 1996 ([Cricinfo guided tour, 1996](https://i.imgci.com/link_to_database/HELPFILES/WWWHELP/GUIDED_TOUR.html)), and the Wisden Almanack is now hosted back to 1864 ([Cricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/online-archive-1864-2025-292356)). Thirty years on, sources still disagree, and no per-scorecard provenance is visible. SCRBRD's importer can do better from its first import. Each imported match would record its source (the photo, the OCR pass, who checked it and when), and each field would carry **known, inferred or unknown**.

Coverage gaps run deep even in the professional game. A third party's dataset built from Cricinfo commentary finds detailed line, length, shot and wagon-wheel attributes for only about **4,000 matches**, line and length mostly from about 2014. Across all balls, line and length are recorded for about **47.5%** and shot for about **74.9%** ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ariadaikalam/the-ultimate-ball-by-ball-cricket-dataset)). These are a third party's measurements, not Cricinfo's own. Even so, if a paid staff with broadcast feeds leaves half the length field empty, volunteers scoring on phones will leave more. Three rules follow. Rich fields stay **optional, with an explicit "not recorded" state** that is never shown as zero. Rates are computed only over balls where the input is known, labelled "n of N balls recorded". And each match carries a **coverage tier** (live ball-by-ball from the pad, over-by-over from an imported scorebook, or scorecard or result only) that decides which charts and records it may feed. Mandatory capture stays limited to the events the Laws need.

Below international level Cricinfo's depth thins out. Associate competitions such as the CWC Challenge League and Canada's NCL GT20 get "Live scorecard" links ([Cricinfo results](https://www.cricinfo.com/live-cricket-match-results)), and the Records section lists Under-19 and "minor cricket" ([Cricinfo statistics](https://www.espncricinfo.com/ci/content/stats/index.html)). No source states a coverage policy by tier, and no evidence shows any junior school coverage. Most of the game's history exists only as scorecards: CricketArchive holds about 900,000 of them, and has been paid-only since 2017 ([CricketArchive](https://cricketarchive.co.uk/index.html); [Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/CricketArchive)). A scorecard-only school match should therefore sit on the same page as a live one, honestly labelled, with ball-level charts withheld.

The derived metrics teach a lesson about explanation. Superstats launched in March 2019, built with IIT Madras and Gyan Data. Its Smart Stats adjust runs and wickets for conditions, opposition and pressure and roll them up into an Impact score; its Luck Index values events such as dropped catches in runs; and Forecaster gives win probability ball by ball ([Cricinfo, Launching Superstats](https://www.cricinfo.com/story/launching-superstats-the-new-language-for-cricket-analysis-1178276); [ESPN explainer](https://www.espn.com/espn/print?id=26312449)). Cricinfo's own FAQ concedes that Smart Stats **does not account for fielding**. Cricinfo says Forecaster picked the winner about **60%** of the time at the start of an IPL chase, rising to about **80%** by the 15th over, but that figure is self-reported and was never independently checked ([InsideSport](https://www.insidesport.in/espncricinfo-launches-new-tool-to-calculate-probabilities-in-cricket/)). The worked-example articles work as explanation. The main criticism is that Impact sits behind "a rather opaque wall – the guarded algorithms" and has had little uptake in the game's everyday language ([Flashscore](https://www.flashscore.com/news/cricket-the-ashes-relative-runs-a-new-way-of-finding-value-in-cricket-s-batting-statistics/0bjwDREd)). Any SCRBRD figure derived from a child's balls, whether a pressure rating or the ground display's par line (SCRBRD-133), should **publish its formula and inputs**, be recomputable from the event log, and recompute when an amendment lands.

Licensing is the last data lesson. ESPN closed its public API (secondary sources give 2014 or 2018; **the dates conflict**), and Cricinfo's data now circulates through unofficial endpoints and scrapers that carry no right to commercial use ([App Developer Magazine](https://appdevelopermagazine.com/the-world-wide-leader-in-sports-leaves-developers-in-a-world-of-hurt-as-espn-closes-public-api/); [APISports](https://apisports.net/providers/espncricinfo); [GitHub scraper](https://github.com/ahmedmuqaddas/ESPNCricInfo-BallByBall)). Where an official channel is missing, scrapers fill the gap. If SCRBRD ever exposes data, a documented, permissioned API that never carries a minor's identity is safer than leaving the public pages to be scraped.

## Every platform found shows children's names by default

Cricinfo offers no model for treating children's data safely. It runs full hubs for each ICC Under-19 World Cup, and a "South African Schools" team page for the representative XI ([Cricinfo U19 WC 2023/24](https://www.espncricinfo.com/series/icc-under-19-world-cup-2023-24-1399722); [Cricinfo: South African Schools](https://www.cricinfo.com/team/south-african-schools-3321/match-schedule-fixtures-and-results)). On them, teenagers get the professional treatment: features describing "the 17-year-old legspinner", player pages, and squad lists that Wikipedia sources for birth dates ([Cricinfo features](https://www.espncricinfo.com/cricket-features?object=7); [Wikipedia, 2016 U19 squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Under-19_Cricket_World_Cup_squads)). No Cricinfo policy on minors' birth dates, photos or consent turned up. This is journalism about elite representative cricket, and it rests on a different legal footing from a school's own system. SCRBRD should still expect some pilot pupils to have public Cricinfo pages one day, and should not feed that publicity.

South Africa's school tier does not run on Cricinfo at all. CSA's U19 Khaya Majola Week is scored on **CricClubs under a "CSA Schools" club, with public ball-by-ball** ([CricClubs: CSA Schools](https://cricclubs.com/CSASchools/listMatches.do?teamId=189&league=13&clubId=29335)). CSA also has its own scoring app ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.cricket.csa&hl=en_US)). And **SuperSport Schools** films and streams school sport automatically on Pixellot cameras, free in its app, on DStv channel 216 and on SABC Plus ([SuperSport Schools](https://supersportschools.com/)). Those are SCRBRD's real neighbours. The table sets out how they and the overseas federation platforms treat a junior's name.

| Platform | Who runs it | A junior's name on public pages | Who can hide it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Play-Cricket | ECB | Shown; admin may set a "display name" "for safeguarding purposes"; under-13s have no own login, record sits on a parent's account | Club admin ([Play-Cricket](https://play-cricket.ecb.co.uk/hc/en-us/articles/115005866765-Player-Names-On-Play-Cricket); [parent account](https://play-cricket.ecb.co.uk/hc/en-us/articles/360001098418-How-to-add-a-child-to-a-parent-s-account)) |
| PlayHQ | CA, NZC, Cricket Scotland | Shown unless set to "Private Player"; **default is contested** between sources | Guardian, for dependants ([PlayHQ](https://support.playhq.com/hc/en-us/articles/23971940444572-Private-Profile-settings-for-Participants)) |
| CricClubs (hosts CSA Schools) | Private SaaS | Profile content "becomes public"; minors need parental consent; 13 and under should not use it | Not documented ([CricClubs privacy](https://cricclubs.com/privacyPolicy.do)) |
| CricHeroes | Private, freemium | Self-serve; under-18 use "must be supervised by a guardian" | Not documented ([CricHeroes privacy](https://cricheroes.com/privacypolicy)) |
| SCRBRD | The school | **Never shown**; no photo either | Not applicable: private for every child |

The pattern is clear. Across the industry, public is the default and privacy is a switch someone has to find and turn on. **PlayHQ's "Private Player"** is the closest precedent and a useful one. The guardian owns the switch, administrators still see the name, and public pages show a placeholder so the scorecard still reads correctly. SCRBRD reverses the default and can borrow the placeholder ("Batter 3"). Play-Cricket's line, with no login of their own for under-13s and the record managed through a parent, is a sensible age line, because POPIA sets no digital age of consent and turns on legal competence instead ([Bird & Bird](https://childreninthedigitalworld.twobirds.com/home/south-africa)). Volunteers who come from CricClubs or Play-Cricket will expect names on the scorecard, and someone will say "the names are already public on CricClubs." The answer is that SCRBRD is the school's system and the school is the responsible party. A pupil's existing publicity elsewhere is not consent for SCRBRD to mirror it, so nothing from CSA Schools, CricClubs or SuperSport Schools should flow into SCRBRD's public views without the same consent gate. A link from a SCRBRD moment to a SuperSport Schools clip would tie a child's identity to footage, so it needs that gate too.

The governing norms back SCRBRD and give it concrete benchmarks. **POPIA s34 prohibits processing a child's personal information** unless a s35 ground applies, normally prior consent from a competent person ([POPIA s35](https://www.acts.co.za/protection-of-personal-information-/35__general_authorisation_conc)). The Information Regulator's 2021 guidance note adds that a guardian must have "a reasonable means … to review the processing and refuse further processing", which is revocable consent in all but name ([Guidance Note, 2021](https://lawlibrary.org.za/akn/za/doc/guidance-note/inforegsa/2021-06-01/guidance-note-on-processing-of-personal-information-of-children/eng@2021-06-01/source)). CSA's own accreditation notice treats **any information about an under-18, and any identifying photo, as special personal information**, processed only with guardian consent, and keeps photos and ID numbers for the **season or event plus 24 months** before deleting them ([CSA Accreditation Privacy Notice](https://accreditation.cricket.co.za/legal/privacy-notice)). That gives the account-lifecycle design a retention default it can cite to schools as the federation's own standard. CSA's Safeguarding Policy includes annexures for applications to photograph or interview children and for guardian consent ([CSA Safeguarding Policy](https://cricket.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/CSA-Safeguarding-Policy.pdf)). SA Hockey requires consent from the **child, the guardian and the DSO** before press photography ([SAHA](https://sahockey.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/SAHA-Safeguarding-policy-updated-29082023-V1.pdf)), and Cricket Australia requires written guardian consent before livestreaming children and screens the people who film them ([CA Safeguarding Policy, 2024](https://resources.playcommunity.pulselive.com/playcommunity/document/2024/11/27/9ddd3384-e1e0-4488-9d3a-dc25eaeefa71/Australian-Cricket-s-Policy-for-Safeguarding-Children-Young-People.pdf)). Any future SCRBRD photo or video feature should therefore go through the DSO, not rest on a parent toggle alone. The UK ICO's Children's Code is not South African law, but its defaults, **high privacy, geolocation off and profiling off**, are the clearest benchmark for SCRBRD's lift clubs, its intelligence feed and its compare-and-combine views ([Freeths](https://www.freeths.co.uk/insights-events/legal-articles/2026/ico-launches-scrutiny-of-children-s-privacy-practices-in-mobile-gaming/)).

One gap in the federations matters here. Their rules govern **images and streaming, not names**, and none of the sources found says a name must be suppressed. Suppressing names is SCRBRD's own, stricter policy. That is a selling point to parents, and SCRBRD will have to defend it to its peers. The breach record points to where the real risk sits. Cricket's incidents happened in **satellite systems**, not core scorers: ECB's third-party coaching site icoachcricket exposed about **43,000** accounts in 2024; CricketSocial left an open database holding dates of birth and addresses; and the BCCI's misconfigured cloud storage exposed player registrations, including U19 categories ([Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/Breach/ECB); [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/cricket-platform-exposed-user-data/); [GBHackers](https://gbhackers.com/bcci-data-leak/)). No breach or regulator action against Play-Cricket, PlayHQ, CricHeroes or CricClubs was found, and no SA Information Regulator action involving a sports platform either. SCRBRD's incidents-and-breaches design should therefore look hardest at Firebase, the importer's OCR provider and any streaming partner.

## School cricket cannot be paid for by viewers or by ads

Each business model in the sector closes off one option for SCRBRD. Cricinfo is ad-funded, and the ads are now its users' most-cited complaint. One user asked for an ad-free tier for ESPN+ subscribers, and none was found ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/417408017/cricinfo-live-cricket-scores/reviews)). Cricbuzz sells an ad-free tier, Cricbuzz Plus, launched in 2021, though deal sites quote prices that conflict ([bestmediainfo](https://bestmediainfo.com/2021/04/cricbuzz-launches-cricbuzz-plus-a-one-stop-destination-for-cricket-coverage)). CricHeroes shows where a free consumer app ends up. With **over 40 million registered users** by a third party's count, it struggled to turn engagement into revenue, and leaned on programmatic advertising, growing ad revenue **400% by June 2025** ([PrivateCircle](https://blog.privatecircle.co/cricheroes-private-limited-powering-the-digital-transformation-of-grassroots-cricket/); [AnyMind](https://anymindgroup.com/case-study/how-cricheroes-grew-advertising-revenue-400-with-anymind-group/)). For SCRBRD, POPIA s34–35 and a profiling-off stance rule out behavioural advertising to child users. A Pro tier sold to pupils would have minors paying for profiling of their own data. The only safe forms are a sponsor the school controls, shown in context with no targeting, and premium features sold to the guardian or the school.

Viewers will not pay either. SuperSport Schools gives school video away free, subsidised by a broadcaster within MultiChoice ([SuperSport Schools](https://supersportschools.com/)), so SCRBRD cannot charge parents to watch. The models that suit it are the ones that bill an organisation. **CricClubs** sells league portals at a free Basic tier, **USD 100** (Essentials) and **USD 300** (Premium), with score overlays from **USD 8 a match** and a free scoring app ([CricClubs pricing](https://cricclubs.com/pricing.do)). **PlayHQ** builds its fee into national registration: a 2023/24 Queensland document shows **AUD 4.80** of each registration going to PlayHQ, and the pricing page lists per-registration rates of AUD 10, 8 or 6. A CA support page gives different service and merchant percentages, and **the two conflict** ([Queensland Cricket](https://westsdcc.cricket/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/QC-PlayHQ-Training-Player-Information-Summer-23-24-3.pdf); [PlayHQ pricing](https://get.playhq.com/pricing); [CA: PlayHQ fees](https://playcricketsupport.cricket.com.au/hc/en-us/articles/13062834071055-PlayHQ-Fees)). For SCRBRD that points to a per-school or per-team licence now, and a union or CSA Schools contract later.

A federation contract brings distribution, but New Zealand shows what a forced switch costs. When NZC moved grassroots cricket from CricHQ to PlayHQ in 2022/23, clubs called the launch a "shambles". Tablet scoring, DLS, leaderboards and player stats were missing, the scoreboards and iPads clubs had bought for CricHQ no longer worked, and some associations stayed with CricHQ or went back to it ([Stuff](https://www.stuff.co.nz/nz-news/350499921/cricket-kiwi-grassroots-players-vent-frustrations-as-new-scoring-system-labelled-shambles-as-season-begins); [NZ Herald](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/sport/community-cricket-lashes-out-over-nz-crickets-new-scoring-system/SA23CV75YLUURCZQA2QZECONA4/)). SuperSport Schools rates **2.3/5** across more than 250,000 downloads, and its reviews complain of streams "constantly lagging" ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/supersport-schools/1563944026)). Both cases make the same point as Cricbuzz's lead over Cricinfo. In this sector reliability decides adoption: lost balls, stalled scores and stranded hardware cost more goodwill than missing features. A school that may later need a provincial union to adopt SCRBRD should be able to import from and export to CricClubs and the CSA app, with the consent gate applied at the boundary.

Several things remain unknown. Cricinfo's own revenue mix was not researched, and the audits do not cover it either. CricHeroes' revenue estimates range from about USD 7.4M to USD 13.4M and do not agree ([Latka](https://getlatka.com/companies/cricheroes.in); [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/cricheroes/__S6PT8SSH2YRY3i03XN6SuL-W3mBdf8EvgxWWXzBz_iU)). The CSA app's commercial model and SuperSport Schools' contracts with schools were not found. **No survey of parents' privacy preferences for cricket or junior-sport apps exists in the sources found**, so SCRBRD's pilot should measure its own: how many guardians consent, and how many choose to show names.

## Conclusion

The pattern across all three areas is that Cricinfo's gaps are SCRBRD's openings. The world's best-known cricket site does not tell readers when a scorecard changed, does not say how much of a match was actually recorded, does not publish the formula behind its headline metrics, and profiles a 17-year-old as readily as a Test captain. SCRBRD's existing designs (amendments with an audit trail, coverage-aware charts, the public-data rule and guardian consent) are therefore not catch-up work. They are a level of trust no major cricket platform found here offers, and that trust is the thing to sell to schools and parents, ahead of breadth, volume or prediction.

The harder problems lie at SCRBRD's edges, not in its core. Pupils' names and video are already public on CricClubs and SuperSport Schools, Opta holds CSA's official data, and the sector's breaches happened in vendors. So the next decisions for Kameel are about boundaries: whether a provincial union or CSA Schools will accept a feed with names suppressed, how imports and exports cross the consent gate, and which vendors sit inside the incident plan. None of the evidence here settles those questions. Every source behind this report should be re-read in full, especially the PlayHQ default and the CSA notice, before either becomes a design rule.
